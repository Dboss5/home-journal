Scratching sounds or droppings can prompt a quick search for traps. A more useful first question is what an inspection can establish about activity and access. For a DFW home, a good proposal should explain the evidence, the likely routes, and the work required to address the problem.

## Record evidence without disturbing it

Photograph visible signs from a safe position and note where and when they appear. Do not enter an unsafe attic, reach into wall voids, or disturb contaminated material to identify it. Keep children and pets away from affected areas and ask the professional about appropriate cleanup.

[Supremacy Service's guide to signs of rats or mice](https://supremacyservice.com/signs-of-rats-or-mice-in-your-dallas-home/) is a relevant starting point for describing what you have observed. Sounds alone do not confirm the species or the extent of activity.

## Ask what the inspection covers

Have the provider identify accessible areas, inaccessible areas, and any additional access needed. Ask whether the proposal includes an exterior entry-point assessment, interior evidence, removal or control measures, exclusion, cleanup, and follow-up. These are distinct tasks and may be priced separately.

The [EPA's rodent prevention guidance](https://www.epa.gov/rodenticides/identify-and-prevent-rodent-infestations) describes reducing accessible food, water, and shelter and addressing entry openings. Those measures support prevention, but a provider still needs to explain how the plan fits your building.

## Require an exclusion plan, not just a list of holes

Ask which openings will be treated, what materials will be used, and how required ventilation and drainage will remain functional. The sequence matters: the provider should explain how activity inside the building will be managed before access points are closed.

For that service discussion, use [Supremacy Service's rodent control and exclusion page](https://supremacyservice.com/rodent-control-and-exclusion-dfw/). Request property-specific findings rather than assuming every gap needs the same treatment.

## Clarify cleanup and damage repair

Ask who handles contaminated materials and whether cleanup is included. If insulation, wiring, or building materials are damaged, request an explanation of the separate repair scope. Rodent removal does not automatically include restoration of everything affected.

Avoid using poisons or placing traps in inaccessible areas as an experiment. Any treatment should follow product instructions and the professional's plan, with children and pets protected from access.

## Define the follow-up

A useful report documents the evidence, treated entry points, limitations, and next inspection. Ask what would count as continuing activity and how to report it. Keep photos and reports together so a later visit can distinguish new signs from material already present at the first inspection.
