Warm-white rooflines, wrapped trees, and pathway lighting all depend on a less visible part of the design: how the display receives power. Before comparing the number of bulbs or the color of the light, map the intended locations and the equipment already using the yard.

For North Texas homes with pools, irrigation controls, or outdoor living areas, that plan should protect access and avoid casual changes to existing circuits.

## Make a location list

Record the roofline sections, trees, walkways, and entry features you want illuminated. Photograph available outdoor receptacles from a safe position. Note the proximity of pool equipment, doors, gates, drainage routes, and places where cords would cross a walking path.

Do not remove a panel cover or assume two outlets are on different circuits because they are far apart. If the circuit arrangement is unclear, ask an electrician to assess it.

## Ask what the product ratings allow

Give the installer the actual product information rather than an approximate bulb count. Ask which components are rated for outdoor use, how the manufacturer's connection limits will be followed, and how cords and connections will be protected from water and physical damage.

GFCI protection is an important part of outdoor electrical safety, but it does not make an overloaded or damaged installation acceptable. Do not bypass a protective device or repeatedly reset it to keep a display on. [Supremacy Service's electrical safety inspection page](https://supremacyservice.com/electrical-safety-inspections-dfw/) is relevant if receptacle condition or circuit suitability is uncertain.

## Keep the pool and equipment area out of the shortcut

Decorative lighting should not interfere with pool barriers, equipment access, or required clearances. Avoid routing cords across the deck or placing connections where water collects. Keep decorative products and extension leads away from the pool as their instructions require; an electrician should assess placement where electrical rules apply.

Do not borrow power from pool equipment or alter its supply for a seasonal display. If existing equipment behaves abnormally after a power interruption, [Poolburg's power outage resource](https://poolburg.com/pool-after-power-outage/) is a relevant follow-up read. An unexplained protective-device trip warrants diagnosis, not a larger breaker.

## Compare the entire installation scope

A written quote should identify installation areas, product ownership, power arrangements, maintenance during the display period, removal timing, and storage. Ask who responds if a section fails and whether roof access or unusual tree work changes the price.

Our [holiday lighting cost guide](/en/blog/real-cost-holiday-lighting) helps structure a scope comparison; ask for current site-specific pricing rather than treating an example as a guaranteed package.

## Check the surroundings before installation day

Arrange repairs for damaged outlets or signs of moisture before lights go up. Use the [water leak guide](/en/blog/pool-or-plumbing-leak-dfw) if wet ground has an unexplained source. If nesting material is visible near the power or equipment area, review [rodent activity around pool equipment](/en/blog/rodents-around-pool-equipment-dfw) and arrange inspection.

Keep pets and children away from installation work. Have the installer explain any user controls, shutoff procedure, and what to do if a protective device trips.
